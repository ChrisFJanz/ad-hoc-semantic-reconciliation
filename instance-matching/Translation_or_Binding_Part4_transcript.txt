Translation or Binding, Part 4: Keeping the bindings
Transcript of the narration.


FROM PART THREE

Part three ended with two agents that refer to the same components. Each has its own model of one
network and its own names. Between them they hold a set of bindings.

A binding is a pair of handles, one from each agent, with a claim: these two denote one component.
One router. One port.

On the map, this is the cell at the bottom right. Individuals that live in the world are bound one
by one, and kept current. Part three was about the first half of that sentence. This part is about
the second.


THE QUESTION

A binding is valid while both handles still denote the same component. When they no longer do, the
binding is void.

Networks change, and so do the systems that describe them. So the question for this part is simple.
When a binding becomes void, how do the agents find out? And how do they put it right?


TWO KINDS OF CHANGE

Two different things can happen, and they must be kept apart.

First, a handle can come to denote another component. A system renames a handle. A system re-keys
its handles, so that two of them exchange the components they denote. Or a component is
decommissioned, and its handles denote nothing. All of these void bindings.

Second, the facts recorded about a component can change, while its handle still denotes it. An
address is changed. Two ports exchange their addresses. Two cables are exchanged. None of these
voids a binding. The port is still the port it was.


THE SILENT ONE

Some of these changes announce themselves. A renamed handle no longer resolves. The next time the
binding is used, it fails, and everyone knows.

A re-keying does not. Both handles still resolve. Both still answer. An instruction sent through the
binding arrives, and acts on the wrong component.

This is not an exotic case. An interface index, the identifier in the question that opened part
three, is a handle of this kind. A system can assign its indexes again.


WHAT NEEDS NO REASONING

Start with what can be done without any reasoning at all. There are four mechanisms.

A handle that no longer resolves is noticed on use. The identifiers can be compared again, as in the
first step of the matching. The relations that each side records can be carried across the bindings
and compared. And every binding can be put to an observation: data that the network has recorded is
read through both handles, and compared.

On the network of one hundred and fifty bindings, each mechanism was tried on each kind of change. A
renaming and a decommissioning are found by all of them. A re-keying of ports that carry addresses
is found by comparing identifiers.

A re-keying of look-alike ports is not. Those ports carry no address. Comparing identifiers finds
none of the eighty void bindings. Comparing relations finds that something is wrong there, and
cannot say which binding. Observation finds exactly the void ones, and finds where each handle now
belongs.


A STALE RECORD

There is a trap in comparing identifiers again. It assumes that both records are current.

Suppose two ports exchange their addresses, and one side has not yet refreshed its record. The
comparison now pairs each port with the other one's counterpart. It reports two valid bindings as
void. It proposes a repair for each. And each repair is wrong.

In the trial, this happened every time: eighty valid bindings reported, eighty wrong repairs
proposed. An observation, on the same cases, raised no alarm, because it reads the network and not
the record.


ANNOUNCEMENT

The other way to keep bindings current is announcement. The side where a change happens tells the
other side.

This is exact when that side knows what changed. And it knows, when its own system tells it: a
change log, stating what was renamed, what was re-keyed, and what was done to the network.

With a change log on each side, two agents were given a batch of seven changes, which left six
bindings void. In all six recorded runs, every binding ended right. It took two to four turns, and
no observation at all.


WHAT A RECORD CANNOT SAY

Without a change log, a side can still compare its own record before and after. That tells it that
something changed. It does not always tell it what.

Take two ports of one bundle. In one case, the system re-keys their two handles. In the other,
somebody exchanges their two cables. In the record, the two cases are identical: two handles now
show each other's connections.

The first case voids two bindings. The second voids none. From its record alone, the side cannot
know which one happened.


RECORDS ONLY

Here is a recorded run, with two agents that have only their records. The seven changes happen. Six
bindings are void.

The agents do well with most of it. They find the renaming. They put the bindings with changed
addresses to an observation, and clear them. They find the re-keying of the ports that carry
addresses.

Then there are agent B's two look-alike ports. B's record shows their connections exchanged. B does
not put them to an observation. Agent A's record shows nothing there, so A does not look. Both
declare done. Two bindings are wrong, and neither agent knows.

Across six runs, this happened in three.


THE RULE

The remedy is one rule, and it has two parts.

A change in a record is evidence, and not a verdict. So any binding whose handle a change touches is
put to an observation, and must be found to be one component, before its agent may finish.

And the observation itself closes the binding. When a pair of handles is observed to be one
component, that pair becomes the binding, whichever side's handles moved. No agent has to work out
who re-keyed what.

Here is the same run, with the rule. B's two bindings are observed: two components, each. The agents
observe the crossed pairs: one component, each. The bindings are set, and settled.


THE RESULT

With the rule, every binding ended right in all six runs.

The cost was about twenty-one observations per run. And it was the same on the network of fifty
bindings as on the network of one hundred and fifty. The work follows the size of the change, and
not the size of the network.


AN INERT SIDE

Now take away the agent on one side. System B only publishes its record.

The one agent that remains made no wrong repair, in any run. But where B's record was ambiguous, it
could only say so, and leave the binding open. In four runs of six, one or two bindings stayed open.

Nothing can be read through the handles of an inert side. So nothing can close them. A binding left
open is known, and can be withheld from use. That is far better than a wrong one. But it stays open.


KEEPING, IN ORDER

So the method for keeping bindings has an order, as the method for making them did.

Announce what the system reports. Where there is no report, treat a change in a record as evidence.
Check every binding that a change touches, and let the check close it. Leave open what cannot be
settled. And between changes, sweep by comparing identifiers and relations, but only with records
known to be current.


BACK TO THE MAP

Part three asked which router we are talking about. This part asked whether we are still talking
about it.

The answer has the same shape both times. What the records determine needs no check. What they
cannot determine goes to the network. And the agents can know, in advance, which is which.

A binding is made once. Keeping it is what makes it safe to use.
